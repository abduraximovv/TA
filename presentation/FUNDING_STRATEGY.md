---

## 1. Read this part first

**The offer on the table — $10,000 for 50% of income — would very likely destroy the company, and it would do so while you are growing.**

That is not a figure of speech. Here is the arithmetic, using a realistic cost structure of 60% of revenue:

| Your revenue | Your costs (60%) | Investor takes (50% of revenue) | **You are left with** |
|---|---|---|---|
| $50,000 | $30,000 | $25,000 | **−$5,000** |
| $100,000 | $60,000 | $50,000 | **−$10,000** |
| $200,000 | $120,000 | $100,000 | **−$20,000** |
| $400,000 | $240,000 | $200,000 | **−$40,000** |

**Every row is negative, and the loss gets bigger as you succeed.** This is the defining property of a revenue share taken off the top: it is senior to your costs. You pay him before you pay Supabase, before you pay the developer, before you eat. There is no revenue level at which this deal becomes survivable — growing faster just means losing money faster.

50% of *income* is not 50% of the company. It is worse. An equity partner who owns half only gets paid when there is profit or an exit. A revenue-share partner at 50% gets paid whether or not you can afford it.

And it does not stop. Over a plausible three-year ramp he collects roughly **$337,000 on a $10,000 cheque — about 34× — and continues collecting forever.**

**One more thing, and it may matter most:** a company that has permanently assigned half its revenue to someone else is effectively unfundable. No grant body, no accelerator, no future investor, and quite possibly no government partner will put money into a business where half of every dollar leaves immediately. Signing this does not just cost you money — it closes every other door in this document, including the ministry conversation you have already built a deck for.

| **Full** ($900 each + contractor) | ~$37,000 | ~$74,000 | $155,131 |

**Monthly burn in the Base case: $1,800.**

### The uncomfortable part

You asked for $20–30k for a project you correctly say needs 2–3 years. **Those two things do not fit together.**

- $10,000 → **5.6 months**
- $30,000 → **16.7 months**
- $40,000 → **22.2 months**
- $87,000 → 36 months

$30k buys you *one season*, not three. Your own seasonality argument — miss the season and you wait a year — is exactly why a 12-month raise is dangerous: if you are 2 months late, you run out of money precisely when you most need to keep going.

**So do one of two things, and be deliberate about which:**

**Option A — raise the real number.** Ask for $85–90k against a 36-month plan. Harder from a single local investor, realistic from grants plus government plus a small angel round combined.

**Option B — raise a deliberate 18-month bridge (~$45k) and say so.** Enough to reach the 2027 season *and* survive missing it. Explicitly framed as a bridge to the government funding and the revenue you will have started earning by then. This is the more honest and more fundable story, and I would lean toward it.

What you should not do is raise $30k while telling people it covers three years. Anyone competent will check, and the number does not survive checking.

## 4. What to counter with

Your investor is a local businessman making his first startup investment. He is almost certainly not trying to destroy you — he is anchoring on "half the business for half the money I have," because percentages are intuitive and startup instruments are not. **This is very negotiable, and you should assume good faith while being completely firm on the structure.**

Ranked, best to worst for you:

### Option 1 — Capped revenue share *(the easiest sell)*
**7% of revenue until he has received 2.0× his money, then it ends.**

| Year | He receives | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|
| 2027 | $1,750 | $1,750 |
| 2028 | $10,500 | $12,250 |
| 2029 | $7,750 | $20,000 — **cap reached, payments stop** |

He doubles his money in about three years. You keep 100% of the company. This works because it speaks his language — a percentage of revenue — while replacing the two terms that are lethal: the *rate* (7%, not 50%) and the *cap* (it ends). Market revenue-based financing sits at roughly 7% with a 1.3–2.0× cap, so this is not you being clever, it is you being normal.

### Option 2 — Convertible loan
$10k converts to equity at your next priced round with a 20% discount. If that round values you at $300k he gets ~4.2%; at $750k, ~1.7%. Nobody has to argue about what the company is worth today, which is the argument you cannot win yet.

### Option 3 — Straight loan
Repay $10k plus interest over 24 months. Boring, clean, keeps everything. Good if what he actually wants is a safe return rather than a stake in a startup.

### Option 4 — Equity, properly priced
Only if he insists on ownership. Look at what different percentages imply:

| He asks for | Implied value of everything you have built |
|---|---|
| 5% | $200,000 |
| 10% | $100,000 |
| 20% | $50,000 |
| **50%** | **$20,000** |

You have four working portals, a live database, authentication, real-time bookings, a review system, a brand, and 23 slides of government-ready positioning. **$20,000 is not a valuation. It is a rounding error.** If he wants equity, 5–10% for $10k is the conversation.

### How to say it

Do not argue about fairness — argue about arithmetic, and let him reach the conclusion. Something close to:

> "I want you involved, and I want you to make good money on this. But I modelled 50% of revenue and it doesn't work mechanically — at $100k of revenue our costs are $60k, so after your 50% we're at minus $10k. The company would go under while growing, and you'd lose your investment with it. Here's a structure that pays you 2× and doesn't break us."

That framing lets him say yes without losing face, because you are protecting *his* return, not just yours. That is usually the difference between a renegotiation and a fight.

Ranked by cost to you. Notice that the cheapest capital is at the top and the private investor is near the bottom.

4. **Revenue, starting now.** See §6 — this is the one people forget.
5. **A small, properly structured angel round** — your businessman, on Option 1 or 2 terms, ideally alongside one or two others so no single person dominates.
6. **Uncapped revenue share or cheap equity** — last resort, and only with terms from §4.

## 6. You may be able to earn this money instead of selling half your income for it

From `MONETIZATION_STRATEGY.md`: agency SaaS needs **no payment licence, no government agreement and no consumer scale.** The CRM, live inventory and booking flows are already built and verified.

**10 agencies × $100/month = $12,000/year.**

That is more than the $10,000 on the table. For ten customers. In a country where, by your own PRD, agency managers spend 60% of their time on exactly the manual work your portal eliminates.

This changes the negotiation completely. Every paying agency you sign before you talk numbers again does two things: it reduces how much you need to raise, and it raises what your company is worth. Ten paying customers turns "$10k for 50%" into a conversation you are no longer desperate to have.

**Concretely, before you sign anything: try to sign three paying agencies.** Even at a discounted $50/month, three paying customers proves the model, and it is the single highest-leverage thing you could do in the next month.
